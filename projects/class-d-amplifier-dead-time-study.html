Class-D Amplifier Dead-Time Study

Simulating how a Class-D amplifier's switching safety gap affects audio distortion.

Timeframe
August 2026
Tools used
LTspice (circuit simulation, PWM modulation, Fourier/THD analysis)
Category
Power Electronics, Circuit Simulation
Build type
Simulation only (LTspice) — not built IRL

The problem

Class-D amplifiers power almost every battery-driven audio device (phones, wireless speakers, laptops) because they're far more power-efficient than older amplifier designs — they work by rapidly switching between two voltage rails rather than smoothly scaling voltage. But the two switches involved can never be on simultaneously, or they'd short-circuit the power supply. To prevent this, engineers insert a small forced pause called "dead-time" between switching transitions. This project investigates a real design tradeoff: how does the size of that safety gap affect audio quality (measured as total harmonic distortion), and is that relationship a simple straight-line tradeoff or something more complex?

Methodology

Built a simplified half-bridge Class-D output stage in LTspice, with PWM modulation generated by comparing a 1kHz audio signal against a 50kHz triangle carrier wave. Dead-time was implemented directly in the comparator logic and swept from 5ns to 200ns in 5ns increments (40 total simulation runs). At each dead-time value, LTspice's Fourier analysis tool measured THD at the filtered output.

Process

  • Designed and built the half-bridge switching circuit (two ideal switches, ±15V supply rails, LC output filter).
  • Added real PWM modulation using a comparator, audio sine wave, and triangle carrier.
  • Built dead-time directly into the switching logic.
  • Set up a parameterized sweep (.step) across 40 dead-time values.
  • Automated THD extraction via LTspice's .four Fourier command.
  • Debugged the circuit extensively (floating references, grounding, filter resonance) before trusting the data.
  • Logged and plotted THD vs. dead-time across the full sweep.

Results

Hypothesized that THD would remain roughly flat for small dead-time values, then rise sharply past a threshold. Instead, THD decreased smoothly and monotonically as dead-time increased across the full range (37.18% down to 36.92%) — no flat region, no sharp threshold. This suggests the ideal switch model used doesn't capture the real-world shoot-through risk the original hypothesis was based on.

What I'd do differently

Rebuild the switch model using real MOSFET behavior (with parasitic resistance and real shoot-through current possible) to see if the expected threshold effect appears under more realistic conditions.
